# ADR 0020: HIPAA & Health Data Privacy Compliance

- **Status:** Accepted
- **Date:** 2026-08-23
- **Author(s):** jonetm@gmail.com

> **This ADR is not legal advice.** It records the engineering team's current, non-authoritative understanding and a recommended default posture. The actual legal classification of this data and applicable obligations should be confirmed with legal counsel before launch, and revisited whenever the product's data flows or user base change.

## Context

Beginning with ADR 0014, the app stores nutrition plans and biometric goals/measurements (weight, blood pressure, blood sugar, etc.) — sensitive personal health information. It's tempting to reach immediately for "HIPAA compliance" as the standard to meet, but HIPAA's requirements attach to specific legal roles (**covered entities** — health plans, healthcare clearinghouses, most healthcare providers — and their **business associates**), not simply to any application that stores health-related data. A standalone, direct-to-consumer nutrition/tracking app collecting self-reported data is typically **not** a HIPAA-covered entity by default. That said, several other legal regimes plausibly *do* apply regardless of HIPAA's status, including the FTC's Health Breach Notification Rule (aimed at consumer health-record vendors), state comprehensive health-privacy laws (e.g., Washington's My Health My Data Act, which regulates "consumer health data" broadly, not just covered entities), state consumer-privacy laws treating health data as sensitive (e.g., California's CCPA/CPRA), GDPR if any EU users are served (health data is a "special category" requiring explicit consent), and platform policies (Apple/Google impose their own health-data handling rules independent of law). This ADR sets the team's working posture given that mixed picture.

Critically, this is no longer a purely hypothetical future scenario: **sharing user health data with a healthcare provider is treated as a planned, long-term product requirement** (see ADR 0021 for the interface design), not merely a possibility to revisit "if it ever comes up." That materially changes the calculus here — a data flow from this app to a provider's system is very likely to constitute this app acting as a HIPAA **business associate** to that provider (a covered entity), which is a different and more concrete question than whether the app itself is a covered entity. The safeguards and consent posture below should be built with that eventual data flow in mind from the start, not bolted on reactively once ADR 0021's interface ships.

## Decision

- Treat the precise legal classification (does HIPAA apply; which other regimes apply) as requiring **legal counsel review before launch**, not an engineering-team determination. This ADR is the placeholder for that decision, not a substitute for it.
- Regardless of the eventual legal classification, **voluntarily adopt HIPAA-comparable technical and organizational safeguards** as the baseline for all nutrition-plan, biometric, and health-tracker data: encryption in transit and at rest, strict per-household data access scoping (building on ADR 0006/0007), access audit logging specific to health-category data, a documented retention/deletion policy, and data minimization (collect and sync only what a feature actually needs).
- Treat **user consent and control over health-category data as distinct** from other app data (shopping lists, pricing, coupons): require a clear, specific opt-in before collecting nutrition-plan or biometric data at all, and provide a straightforward way for a user to export or delete their health data. This maps to obligations that plausibly exist under several of the non-HIPAA regimes noted above, independent of whether HIPAA itself applies.
- **Logically separate health-category data** (nutrition plans, biometric goals/measurements) from non-health app data (shopping lists, pricing, coupons) in storage and access control, so stricter safeguards and audits can be applied specifically to health data without over-constraining unrelated features.
- Since sharing data with a healthcare provider is a planned long-term requirement (ADR 0021), treat the eventual provider-sharing integration as **very likely to make this app a HIPAA business associate** for that data flow, and design toward that now rather than waiting until the integration ships to react: budget for a Business Associate Agreement with any provider/EHR platform the sharing interface connects to, and ensure the safeguards in this ADR (encryption, access scoping, audit logging, retention/deletion, data minimization) are already in place for the data categories ADR 0021 will export, before that interface is built. Confirm the specific obligations with legal counsel once ADR 0021's interface design is finalized, since the business-associate analysis depends on the concrete mechanics of that data flow (push export vs. live API access).

## Consequences

- Deferring the precise legal classification to counsel avoids two failure modes: overclaiming HIPAA compliance (a specific, auditable legal status not established merely by trying hard) and underestimating obligations that other laws impose regardless of HIPAA's applicability.
- Voluntarily adopting HIPAA-comparable safeguards raises the engineering bar (encryption, audit logging, access scoping, deletion tooling) earlier than strictly required today, but reduces the risk of a costly retrofit if the user base, integrations, or applicable law change.
- Logically separating health-category data from other app data adds some data-modeling and access-control complexity now, but makes future compliance audits and stricter access rules tractable without touching unrelated subsystems (pricing, coupons, shopping lists).
- Building explicit consent, export, and delete flows for health data is real, non-trivial product/engineering scope, but is very likely required in some form by at least one applicable regulation regardless of HIPAA's specific status, so it is treated as near-term scope rather than deferred indefinitely.
- This ADR should be revisited whenever the product adds a new integration (especially anything healthcare-adjacent) or expands into new jurisdictions, since the applicable-law analysis is integration- and jurisdiction-specific and can change the answer.
- Treating provider data-sharing as a planned requirement rather than a hypothetical means the safeguards here are effectively a prerequisite for ADR 0021, not a parallel, independent concern — the two ADRs should be tracked together, and ADR 0021's interface should not ship ahead of the safeguards and consent model established here.

## Alternatives Considered

| Option | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|
| **Assume HIPAA doesn't apply and skip special handling for health data beyond general app security** | Lowest near-term engineering cost | Real risk of non-compliance with laws that can clearly apply regardless of HIPAA status (FTC Health Breach Notification Rule, state health-privacy laws); reputational/legal exposure if a breach occurs and sensitive health data was handled no differently than a shopping list |
| **Assume HIPAA does apply and pursue full formal HIPAA compliance** | "Safe by over-compliance" instinct | HIPAA's formal compliance program (e.g., Business Associate Agreements) is built around covered-entity relationships that may not even be the correct legal framework here; pursuing it without an actual HIPAA-covered relationship risks misallocating significant compliance effort against the wrong framework instead of the ones that actually apply |
| **Treat health data identically to other app data, with no distinct safeguards, consent, or export/delete tooling** | Simplest data model | Fails to address real legal exposure under non-HIPAA health-privacy laws, and is poor practice given the sensitivity of the data regardless of the precise legal requirement |
